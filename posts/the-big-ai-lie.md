### Salesforce Made a Big Bet on AI

In 2025, Marc Benioff made a pretty big statement.

Salesforce was not planning to hire more software engineers that year.

Why?

AI.

Benioff said AI coding tools had increased engineering productivity by around 30%, so Salesforce did not need to keep adding engineers the way it did before. [The company went ahead with the engineering hiring freeze.](https://www.theregister.com/2025/02/27/salesforce_wont_hire_more_engineers/)

At the time, the message felt simple:

**AI is making engineers so productive that companies will need fewer of them.**

And to be fair, that idea did not sound crazy.

AI was getting better at coding very quickly.

But fast forward to 2026 and there is another part of the story.

Salesforce still has around **15,000 engineers**. Benioff has said the engineering headcount has stayed mostly flat because AI tools helped the existing team become more productive. He has also said that current AI models still cannot fully replace those engineers because they cannot operate on their own. [The engineers are still needed.](https://www.salesforceben.com/ai-cant-replace-software-engineers-yet-marc-benioff-says/)

That difference matters.

The hiring freeze was real.

The productivity gains may also be real.

But neither of those things means the engineer disappeared.

I think the bigger mistake was that we started treating **coding** and **software engineering** as if they were the same job.

They are not.

### AI Can Write Code. That Part Is Real.

There is no point pretending AI coding tools are useless.

They are getting better.

GitHub Copilot made code completion much better. Then ChatGPT made it possible to describe something in normal language and get code back. After that came coding agents that could read files, change code, run commands, and work through larger tasks.

That is real progress.

Today, you can write something like:

> "Create an API for this."

And AI can sometimes give you a good starting point in seconds.

I use AI with code too, and there are times when it saves me a lot of effort.

But this is where I think the whole **AI versus engineers** discussion went wrong.

We started treating software engineers like people whose main job is typing code.

### Writing Code Is Only Part of the Job

Imagine AI creates a feature in ten minutes.

Great.

Now someone still has to ask:

* Does it actually solve the right problem?
* Is the code secure?
* Does it work with the rest of the system?
* Will it break something that already exists?
* What happens when another service fails?
* Can we maintain this code six months from now?
* Who is responsible if it goes wrong?

That last question matters a lot.

In a demo, AI can create something that looks finished.

In a real company, **"looks finished"** and **"is ready for production"** are very different things.

Real systems have old code, strange business rules, security checks, dependencies, different teams, and years of changes sitting on top of each other.

Sometimes even the engineers working on a system do not fully know why one old piece of code exists.

You remove it.

Everything looks fine.

Then something completely different breaks.

Welcome to software engineering.

### This Is Why the Salesforce Example Is Interesting

Salesforce still having around 15,000 engineers does not mean its AI strategy failed.

I think it shows something more useful.

AI may allow those engineers to do more work without Salesforce increasing the size of the team.

That is a serious change.

But it is different from replacing the team.

In early 2026, Benioff said Salesforce's engineering headcount had stayed **mostly flat** at around 15,000 while the engineers had become more productive with coding agents. [He described the existing team as more productive than before.](https://www.itpro.com/business/business-strategy/marc-benioff-salesforce-software-engineering-hiring-flat-ai)

A few months later, he made the limit even clearer: AI can help the engineers, but the models still cannot work fully on their own. [Human engineers are still part of the process.](https://www.salesforceben.com/ai-cant-replace-software-engineers-yet-marc-benioff-says/)

That sounds much closer to what is actually happening.

**AI is changing the job before it is removing the job.**

### Someone Still Has to Check the AI's Work

There is another problem.

AI-generated code is not always good code.

CodeRabbit studied 470 open-source pull requests and reported that AI-generated pull requests had about **1.7 times more issues** than human-written ones. It also found that logic and correctness issues were around **75% more common** in the AI-generated pull requests. [You can read the report here.](https://www.coderabbit.ai/blog/state-of-ai-vs-human-code-generation-report)

That does not mean humans always write better code.

Anyone who has worked with software knows humans can write terrible code too.

The point is simpler.

If AI creates code faster, but humans still have to check, test, fix, and understand that code, then the engineer has not disappeared.

The work has changed.

Instead of writing every line yourself, maybe more of your time goes into reviewing what AI created and deciding whether it belongs in the system.

And that changes what companies should look for in engineers.

### Even Productivity Is Not That Simple

There is another interesting example.

In 2025, METR studied experienced developers working on codebases they already knew.

The developers expected AI to make them faster.

Instead, the study found they took **19% longer** when using AI tools. The developers still believed AI had made them faster. [METR published the study in July 2025.](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)

That result got a lot of attention.

But the story did not stop there.

In February 2026, METR published newer data and said the results were becoming harder to measure because developers were less willing to work without AI. In the newer sample, the results were closer to flat, with some signs that AI might now be speeding developers up. [METR explained the change in its follow-up.](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)

And I actually like this example because it shows how messy this discussion is.

AI can make one developer faster on one task and slow another developer down on another.

So when someone says:

**"AI makes developers 30% more productive."**

My next question is:

**Doing what?**

Writing new code?

Debugging an old system?

Reviewing a pull request?

Understanding a production issue?

Building something you already know well?

The answer matters.

### I Don't Think AI Is Going Away

I also do not want to make the opposite mistake.

AI will change software engineering.

A lot.

Some work that junior engineers used to do may become automated.

Teams may become smaller.

Companies may expect each engineer to produce more.

Some roles may disappear, and other roles may appear.

Engineers who completely ignore AI tools may also find themselves falling behind engineers who know how to use them well.

That is already a much more useful discussion than saying:

**"Software engineers are finished."**

There is a big difference between:

**AI will change software engineering.**

and

**AI will remove the need for software engineers.**

The first one is already happening.

The second one is much harder.

### Maybe We Were Asking the Wrong Question

For the last few years, one question has been everywhere:

**Will AI replace software engineers?**

Maybe we were asking the wrong question.

Maybe the better question is:

**What happens when every software engineer has AI working with them?**

Because that seems much closer to where we are going.

AI can write code.

It can explain code.

It can create tests.

It can review pull requests.

It can search through large codebases.

It can help debug problems.

And it will probably become much better at all of these things.

But someone still needs to understand the system.

Someone still needs to know when the AI is wrong.

And someone still needs to take responsibility when things break.

Salesforce froze engineering hiring because AI was helping its engineers produce more.

That part happened.

But in 2026, Salesforce still had around 15,000 engineers, and Benioff was still saying those engineers were needed.

That does not mean AI lost.

It means the story was probably never:

**AI instead of engineers.**

It was:

**Engineers working differently because of AI.**

And I think that tells us more about the future of software engineering than all those **"AI is coming for your job"** headlines ever did.
